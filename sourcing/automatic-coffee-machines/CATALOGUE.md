> ⚠️ **On the $74 "bean-to-cup" (Ningbo Wasai, MOQ 1,000).** A genuine bean-to-cup machine needs a
> grinder, a brew group, a pump and a boiler. Those parts alone cost more than $74 at any credible
> quality. This is almost certainly a drip or pod machine listed under a bean-to-cup keyword to catch
> search traffic — a routine practice on these platforms. **Do not budget from this number.** Demand
> the exploded parts diagram and a video of it grinding whole beans before you treat it as real.

## Reading the price floor honestly

The cheapest unit is almost never the cheapest machine to own. Three things move the real number
more than the sticker price does:

1. **Voltage.** Everything here defaults to 220–240 V / 50 Hz. A US-voltage version may carry a
   surcharge or may not exist at all. A $666 machine you cannot plug in costs infinity.
2. **Certification.** UL/ETL plus NSF costs **$3,000–$15,000** if the supplier does not already hold
   it for that exact model. Amortised over 10 units, that is $300–$1,500 per machine — it can double
   the cost of the cheap end and is nearly irrelevant at the top.
3. **Spare parts and failure rate.** Brew groups, grinder burrs, pumps and boilers are consumables on
   an automatic machine. A supplier with no priced parts list is selling you a disposable machine.
   Get the parts list before comparing prices, and add it to the landed cost.

**Practical shortlist to quote first**, balancing price against the risk above:

|---|---|

| Commercial staffed locations | **GGMGASTRO** ($731.50, MOQ 10) · **Yile** ($1,000–1,040) · **Dr. Coffee F11** ($1,130) · **Evoacas** ($1,300) |
