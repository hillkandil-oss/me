# Watch Size Guide: Finding the Right Fit

> Original Journal content (Watch Education). ~950 words.
> **URL:** `/journal/watch-size-guide/`

The single most common sizing mistake is judging a watch by one number — the case diameter —
when fit actually depends on several dimensions working together. This guide explains the
measurements that matter and how to match them to your wrist.

## Measure your wrist first

Wrap a soft tape measure (or a strip of paper you then measure) around your wrist just below
the wrist bone, where a watch sits. Most adult wrists fall between roughly 15 and 20 cm. As a
rough starting point:

- **15–16 cm (smaller):** 36–39mm cases tend to sit best.
- **16.5–18 cm (medium):** 38–42mm is a comfortable, versatile range.
- **18 cm and above (larger):** 42mm and up can be balanced.

Treat these as a starting point, not a rule — proportion and taste matter, and the numbers
below refine the picture.

## Case diameter

Diameter is the width of the case, excluding the crown. It's the headline figure, but on its
own it's an incomplete guide because two 40mm watches can wear very differently depending on
the dimensions that follow.

## Lug-to-lug: the measurement that decides fit

**Lug-to-lug** is the distance from the tip of the top lugs to the tip of the bottom lugs —
the full length of the watch along your arm. This often matters more than diameter, because it
determines whether the watch sits *within* your wrist or overhangs the edges. If the lug-to-lug
exceeds the flat width of your wrist, the watch will look and feel too large no matter how
modest its diameter. When a spec sheet lists it, read it carefully; it's the number that most
reliably predicts a good fit.

## Thickness

**Case thickness** shapes two things: comfort and formality. A slim watch slides easily under a
shirt cuff and reads as dressy; a thick watch has presence and reads as sporty, but can catch on
sleeves. Dive and chronograph watches are naturally thicker because of what's inside them;
dress watches are prized for being thin. Neither is wrong — match thickness to how and where
you'll wear it.

## Lug width and straps

**Lug width** is the gap between the lugs where the strap attaches, measured in millimetres
(commonly 18, 20, or 22mm). It matters when you want to change straps: a strap must match the
lug width. It also affects proportion — a strap that's too narrow for the case looks
undersized. If swapping straps appeals to you, favour common lug widths for the widest choice.

## Dial and proportion

Two watches of the same diameter can wear differently depending on their dials. A wide bezel
and small dial opening make a watch feel more compact; a thin bezel and large dial make it feel
bigger. This is why trying a watch on, or studying accurate wrist photos, tells you more than
any single number.

## A simple method

1. Measure your wrist width and circumference.
2. Shortlist watches whose **lug-to-lug** fits within your wrist width.
3. Within those, choose a **diameter** in your comfortable range.
4. Match **thickness** to your intended use — slim for dress, thicker for sport.
5. If you'll swap straps, note the **lug width**.

Fit first, fashion second. A watch that's comfortable and well-proportioned on your wrist is a
watch you'll actually wear — and that, in the end, is the point.
