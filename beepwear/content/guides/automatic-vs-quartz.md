# Automatic vs Quartz: Which Movement Is Right for You?

> Original Journal content (Watch Education). ~1,200 words.
> **URL:** `/journal/automatic-vs-quartz/`
> **Meta description:** Automatic vs quartz watches compared — accuracy, maintenance, cost, and character. A clear, side-by-side guide to choosing the right watch movement for you.
> **Author:** BeepWear Editorial Team · **Category:** Watch Education
> **Primary question:** Should I buy an automatic or a quartz watch?

Ask two watch owners which movement is better and you'll often get two confident, opposite
answers. That's because "better" depends entirely on what you want from a watch. Here's how
each works, what it's like to live with, and who each one suits — without the tribalism.

**Quick answer:** Quartz watches are far more accurate (seconds per month vs. seconds per day),
need only an occasional battery, and cost less to own. Automatic (mechanical) watches offer
craftsmanship, tradition, and a self-winding movement you can pass down, but they need regular
wear or a winder and periodic servicing. Choose **quartz** for precision and low fuss; choose
**automatic** for craft and character.

## Automatic vs quartz at a glance

| Factor | Automatic (mechanical) | Quartz (electronic) |
|--------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Power source** | Mainspring wound by wrist motion | Battery + quartz crystal |
| **Accuracy** | A few seconds per day | A few seconds per month |
| **Maintenance** | Service every ~5 years; wear or wind regularly | New battery every 1–2 years |
| **Running cost** | Higher (servicing) | Lower |
| **Upfront cost** | Usually higher at a given quality | Usually lower |
| **Character** | Living mechanism; often a display caseback | Precise, low-fuss, grab-and-go |
| **Best for** | Craft, tradition, heirlooms | Accuracy, convenience, minimal upkeep |

## How does each movement work?

An **automatic** watch is mechanical. A tightly coiled mainspring stores energy and releases
it through a train of gears regulated by a balance wheel that swings back and forth several
times a second — that's the sweeping seconds hand and the faint tick you can sometimes hear.
A weighted rotor winds the mainspring from the motion of your wrist, so an automatic keeps
itself running as long as you wear it.

A **quartz** watch is electronic. A battery sends current through a tiny quartz crystal, which
vibrates at a precise frequency — 32,768 times per second. The movement counts those
vibrations to keep time and pulses the seconds hand once per second, the familiar quartz
"tick." No winding, no rotor, just remarkable accuracy from a battery.

## Is automatic or quartz more accurate?

This one isn't close. A good quartz watch drifts by only seconds per month; a fine mechanical
watch is doing well to stay within a few seconds per *day*. If pinpoint accuracy with zero
effort is your priority, quartz wins decisively.

But mechanical owners rarely choose their watches for accuracy — a few seconds a day is
trivially corrected, and the appeal was never about beating a phone's clock.

## Which needs more maintenance day to day?

- **Automatic:** wear it regularly and it runs; leave it a couple of days and it stops, ready
  to be wound and reset. A watch winder keeps a rotation of automatics running. Every several
  years it should be serviced by a watchmaker — cleaned, oiled, and regulated — which is a real
  ongoing cost but also what lets a mechanical watch run for generations.
- **Quartz:** grab it and go, whether you wore it yesterday or last month. It needs a new
  battery every year or two and little else. Lower fuss, lower running cost.

## Do they feel different to own?

Here's the heart of it. An automatic is a small mechanism you carry through the day — a rotor
you can feel settle, a movement you can often watch through a display caseback, an object with
a sense of being alive. For many owners that craft *is* the point.

A quartz watch makes no such claim and doesn't try to. It's honest, precise, and quietly
excellent at its job. There's a purity to that, too.

## Which movement costs more?

Mechanical movements are more complex to make and finish, so at a given level of quality an
automatic usually costs more than a quartz — and carries the added long-term cost of
servicing. Quartz is more affordable to buy and to own. Neither fact makes one "cheap" or
"premium"; there are exceptional watches of both kinds.

## Which movement suits you?

**Choose automatic if** you value craftsmanship and tradition, enjoy a watch that asks a
little involvement, like the idea of a piece that can be serviced and passed on, and don't
mind resetting it after time off the wrist.

**Choose quartz if** you want maximum accuracy with minimum fuss, prefer to reach for a watch
after weeks in a drawer and have it show the right time, or want fine design without the
running cost of servicing.

Many collectors end up with both: an automatic for the days they want to feel the craft, and a
quartz for the days they just need to go. If you're choosing your first, let your honest
temperament decide — the involved enthusiast and the practical minimalist are both well served.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is automatic or quartz better?**
Neither is universally better. Quartz is more accurate and lower-maintenance; automatic offers
mechanical craft and heirloom appeal. The right choice depends on whether you value precision
and convenience or tradition and character.

**Do automatic watches need a battery?**
No. Automatic watches are powered by a mainspring wound by your wrist's motion, not a battery.
They stop if left unworn for a day or two and simply need winding and resetting.

**How long do automatic and quartz watches last?**
Both can last decades with care. A serviced mechanical watch can run for generations; a quartz
watch keeps going reliably as long as the battery is replaced and the case is resealed properly.

**Is quartz less prestigious than automatic?**
Not inherently. There are exceptional and highly collectible watches of both types. Prestige
comes from the watch's quality, finishing, and heritage — not the movement type alone.
