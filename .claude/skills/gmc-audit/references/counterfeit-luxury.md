# Counterfeit & branded / luxury goods

This is the policy that turns a suspension into a **permanent, often
un-appealable** ban. Google runs a **zero-tolerance** counterfeit policy across
every category, but **luxury and branded goods — watches, handbags, sneakers,
jewelry, sunglasses, fragrances, electronics — get disproportionate scrutiny**
because they're the most-counterfeited products online. A confirmed counterfeit
finding usually means immediate account termination with no second chance.

Critically: a "counterfeit" label **does not require the goods to actually be
fake**. Google flags on *risk signals*. Genuine goods with weak documentation,
implausible pricing, or vague sourcing get swept up too. The audit job is to
remove the signals a reviewer would read as "possibly counterfeit."

## Who may advertise branded goods

- Sellers of **genuine** branded products, and **authorized resellers**, may
  advertise them.
- **Replicas / "inspired by" / "dupe" / "AAA" / "mirror" products are banned** —
  anything copying a brand's design or trademark counts as counterfeit.
- Independent resellers of genuine pre-owned/vintage goods **can** sell, but must
  be able to substantiate authenticity on demand. Never imply an authorized
  relationship you don't have.

## Risk signals to eliminate

1. **Implausible pricing.** Prices far below market with no explanation are the
   #1 counterfeit flag. Genuine pre-owned pricing (varying by condition, year,
   box/papers, service history) is fine and *reduces* suspicion — but a $300
   "Rolex" or a $90 "Louis Vuitton" bag is a near-instant flag. Check the
   feed: for each luxury brand, are prices in a believable range for the
   condition claimed?
2. **Vague or missing sourcing.** An authenticity page that says nothing
   concrete ("sourced through legitimate channels") reads as evasive. State the
   *real* model: authorized distributor, verified private sellers, estate/auction
   acquisitions, trade-ins, etc.
3. **No authentication process described.** For high-value branded goods,
   describe the *actual* inspection/authentication steps performed (reference
   check, serial/movement verification, third-party authentication where used).
   Only claim checks you truly perform.
4. **Trademark misuse.** Using brand logos as if endorsed, brand names in the
   store/domain name implying affiliation, or brand terms stuffed into titles
   deceptively.
5. **Grey-market ambiguity.** Grey-market goods (genuine but outside official
   channels, often no local warranty) are allowed but must be disclosed —
   state warranty status honestly rather than implying full manufacturer
   warranty.

## What the merchant must be ready to produce (owner records)

Keep these retrievable; Google may request proof during review:

- Purchase invoices / receipts from suppliers showing legitimate acquisition.
- Serial / reference / model records per unit where possible.
- Authentication or service documentation (in-house or third-party).
- Provenance for high-value or vintage pieces (box, papers, ownership history).
- Any authorization agreement, if claiming authorized-dealer status.

## Audit checklist — branded / luxury goods

- [ ] No replica/dupe/"inspired-by" products anywhere in the feed or site.
- [ ] Prices are believable for the brand + condition; no unexplained bargains.
- [ ] Authenticity/sourcing page states a real, specific sourcing model.
- [ ] Real authentication/inspection steps described (only ones performed).
- [ ] No implied brand affiliation/authorization that doesn't exist; independent
      reseller status stated plainly.
- [ ] Warranty status (esp. grey-market) disclosed honestly.
- [ ] Owner holds sourcing/authenticity documentation and can produce it.
- [ ] Product images are of the actual item (or its exact reference), not
      generic brand marketing shots that misrepresent what ships.

Even with everything above, expect **manual review** for high-risk brands, and
never promise the merchant approval — luxury feeds are reviewed conservatively.
